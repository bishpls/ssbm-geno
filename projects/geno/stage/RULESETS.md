# Melee's competitive stage list: how it works, and why each stage sits where it does

Research for the Forest Maze stage (DESIGN.md §1 derives the starter criteria from it). Michael's meaning of "starter"
(2026-09-29): **a stage fit for game 1 of a set in the tournament structure: fair, balanced, no severely game-altering
geometry, no strong character favourability.** Numbers in brackets are our own measurements from the disc
(`research/starters.md`), in world units (Final Destination's ledges at ±85.57).

**How this was researched.** SmashWiki pages were read in full on 2026-09-29 (sources at the end). Liquipedia refused
automated fetches (HTTP 403), and this session's web-search budget was spent, so the dated history below is what SmashWiki
documents plus a few items marked *(unsourced)* that we believe but could not cite this round. The per-stage reasons are
SmashWiki's, cross-checked against our measurements.

## 1. How the list works

**Starters and counterpicks.** A *starter* (also called a *neutral*) is a stage that "may be chosen in any game of a set,
including the first game"; a *counterpick* can only be picked after game 1 [S1, S2]. The split exists because game 1 has
no loser yet: its stage has to come from a pool fair enough that neither player needs a remedy.

**Game 1: stage striking.** The players take turns removing starters until one is left, and that one is played [S3]. With
five starters, the usual pattern is 1-2-1: one player strikes one, the other strikes two, the first strikes one more
*(unsourced; the common practice)*. Striking is why a starter list must be *uniformly* fair. Each player strikes their
worst stages, so any stage that strongly favours one archetype gets struck by the opposing player every time and never
gets played. A stage that is never struck is the shared "fairest" stage: Battlefield "is often the first stage that two
players strike or agree to" [S7].

**Games 2 onward: the counterpick.** The loser of the previous game picks the stage, from the whole legal list (starters
and counterpicks) [S4]. The winner may first ban one stage; the current SBR-style ruleset drops that ban in best-of-five
sets [S5]. SmashWiki also notes that best-of-five sets often skip striking, because the pool is small [S3].

**Dave's Stupid Rule (DSR).** "A player cannot counterpick to *any* stage they have won on during the set" [S6].
- **Origin:** Scamp wrote it after Tournament Go 4 (January 2003), having watched Ken roll a random stage and later
  counterpick back to it. Head TO Matt Deezie adopted it and gave it its name [S6].
- **Modified DSR**, the original form, bans only the *last* stage a player won on. It is now rare in Melee.
- **Other variants:** a game-restricted rule (no stage played twice in the set, whoever won) and the Stage Dismissal Rule
  (no counterpick to a stage the player already counterpicked and won on) [S6].
- **Standard DSR is now universal.** The shift accelerated after the Revival of Melee 5 controversy (2012), where
  Mew2King had to replay game 5 over rule confusion [S6].

**Current list** (SmashWiki's SBR ruleset page, read 2026-09-29) [S5, S2]:

| | Singles | Doubles |
|---|---|---|
| Starters | Battlefield, Dream Land, Final Destination, Fountain of Dreams, Yoshi's Story | the same, minus Fountain of Dreams |
| Counterpick | Pokémon Stadium | Pokémon Stadium |
| Format | 4 stocks, 8 minutes; best of 3, best of 5 late in bracket | |

Six legal stages in all; every other stage is banned.

## 2. How the list changed

- **Early days (2002-2004).** Rulesets varied by region, and items were the main dispute. Game Over (January 2004) was
  the earliest major with items off [S5].
  - Japan played Final Destination only for a time, "due to it having no stage hazards and a simplistic design" [S8].
  - Battlefield was legal from the start, "except for early events that only permitted Final Destination" [S7].
- **The wide counterpick era.** SmashWiki lists 15 stages that were legal counterpicks and are now banned: Big Blue,
  Brinstar, Corneria, Green Greens, Jungle Japes, Kongo Jungle, Kongo Jungle 64, Mushroom Kingdom, Mushroom Kingdom II,
  Mute City, Onett, Poké Floats, Princess Peach's Castle, Rainbow Cruise, Yoshi's Island [S1]. Most went "as time went
  on" [S10]: the metagame found the camping, the infinites and the stage-driven KOs each one allowed (§4).
- **Kongo Jungle 64** was the last "retired counterpick" [S2]. A Genesis match between Pink Shinobi and RockCrock is
  "commonly regarded as one of the primary reasons why the stage was eventually banned" [S9]. (Genesis 1 was in 2009
  *(unsourced)*.) It lasted longer in doubles, where four characters make circle camping harder.
- **The six-stage list.** Final Destination, Battlefield, Yoshi's Story, Dream Land, Fountain of Dreams and Pokémon
  Stadium became the whole legal list. Pokémon Stadium was "historically considered one of the fairest stages and
  remained a starter for an extended period" [S11].
- **Frozen Pokémon Stadium (2020 to the mid-2020s).** This is a mod that keeps the stage in its neutral form.
  - It started at regionals (Battle of BC 3), and Slippi netplay used it as the default during the pandemic.
  - After offline events resumed it "became the norm for a few years" [S12].
  - In that era Final Destination became "the only tournament legal stage not available as a starter", a counterpick
    [S8].
- **Nintendo's Community Tournament Guidelines** were published on 24 October 2023 and took effect on 15 November 2023.
  They prohibit "pirated or modified versions of Nintendo games" [S13].
  - Frozen Stadium "has since declined due to legal issues with Nintendo"; "most majors have reverted to the unfrozen
    version" [S12]. Slippi used Frozen Stadium by default until August 2025 [S12].
  - With the transformations back, Pokémon Stadium moved to counterpick, and Final Destination returned to the
    starters [S8, S11].
- **Charity unbans.** In 2024, The Off-Season 2 made Brinstar, Corneria and Mute City legal as charity incentives [S10,
  S14, S15]. Mute City was also unbanned for an Apex 2015 exhibition that was never played [S15]. These are one-offs,
  not changes to the list.
- **Regional variation.** SmashWiki says community preferences differ with "no agreed-upon standard across all regions"
  [S1]. Its detail is mostly for Brawl and Smash 4. The Melee list above has been very stable since the frozen-Stadium
  question was settled.

## 3. Why each legal stage has its status

| Stage | Status | What puts it there (SmashWiki, with our numbers) |
|---|---|---|
| **Battlefield** | Starter | The most neutral stage: "its geometry and platform layout do not give many significant matchup-specific advantages" [S7]. Its one quirk is the **edges**. "The angled walls connected to the ledges make it possible for a character to slip underneath the ledge, before their ledge grab box enters the range that allows for ledge grab" [S7]. A vertical up-B that misses ends "stuck underneath the stage for a likely KO". Fox and Marth have a harder time sweetspotting, and Peach can float under the edge and die; Mewtwo can pull opponents through the thin lip. *Ours:* the side cuts in **13.2 units** at 10 below the ledge, the most of the six (Final Destination 0, Dream Land 0.8). Three platforms at 27.2 / 54.4. |
| **Dream Land** | Starter | "A much higher ceiling" than the N64 original [S16]. The best stage for Peach, Jigglypuff and Samus in some matchups, and a bad one for Marth and the Ice Climbers. The ceiling protects floaty characters from vertical KOs. The size lets Fox and Peach camp the Ice Climbers and escape grab combos. The high platforms stop Marth's ground attacks hitting through them. *Ours:* the biggest blast zones by far (±255, top 250, **177.7** past each ledge), the highest side platforms (30.1 / 30.2, asymmetric by 0.1) and a top platform at 51.4. Whispy's wind is its only hazard. |
| **Yoshi's Story** | Starter | Its only hazards are Randall and the Shy Guys; a "simple yet versatile layout" with "smaller blast zones" favours aggression [S17]. One of Fox's best stages: up smash or up throw to up air kills "at exceedingly low percents". One of Marth's best, and "Bowser's best stage by a significant margin". Hard for Jigglypuff, Peach and Captain Falcon. Randall runs on a predictable 20-second cycle that recoveries can use. *Ours:* the smallest stage (ledges **±56**, 112 wide), the closest blast zones (117.6 / 119.7 past the ledges, top 168, bottom 91), **slanted ledges** 3.5 below the floor, and the lowest platforms (23.45 / 42). |
| **Fountain of Dreams** | Starter (singles; banned in doubles) | "One of the most popular stages for competitive play, due to its general balance" [S18]. The moving side platforms stop defensive camping. The edges' shape stops characters getting stuck under them, and the bottom pillar gives wall-jump recoveries a wall without allowing stalling. Good for Marth and Jigglypuff. The moving platforms can spoil wavedash and waveland timing. Banned in doubles for its size and for frame drops from the water reflection. *Ours:* 126.7 wide, a static top platform at 42.75, and side platforms whose height changes over time (measured in `starters.md`). The deepest bottom blast zone (146.25 below). |
| **Final Destination** | Starter (a counterpick in the Frozen Stadium era) | No platforms [S8]. Chain grabs and long combos: Marth chain grabs fast fallers to low-percent KOs, Fox, Pikachu and Peach have chain grabs, and Falco pillars most characters. "One of the best stages for projectile users" with "no platforms for opponents to escape to". It simplifies recoveries and removes platform movement. Best for the Ice Climbers and Marth; worst for Jigglypuff, Sheik and Yoshi. Critics compare its effect to wobbling. *Ours:* ±85.57, blast zones ±246 / 188 / −140, a vertical wall for 10.5 under each ledge, then the side slopes in. |
| **Pokémon Stadium** | Counterpick (a starter while Frozen) | It favours Fox and Falco too much: "the geometry of the stage and terrain allow them to projectile camp with Blaster very safely" [S11]. The transformations' walls allow Fox's drill-shine infinite. Its "large horizontal size" and low ceiling suit their approaches and kills. Marth and Captain Falcon gain a little from the platforms and dash-dance room, but that "pale[s] in comparison". The Fire and Rock transformations get waited out. *Ours:* the widest stage (ledges **±87.75**, 175.5 wide), two platforms at 25, top blast 180, and four transformation layouts in the file besides the neutral one. |

## 4. Why the formerly legal stages were banned

| Stage | Why it lost its status [source] |
|---|---|
| Kongo Jungle 64 | **Circle camping.** "Peach and Jigglypuff were found to be very powerful on the stage, as the platform layout made it extremely difficult to approach floaty characters circle camping across the two top platforms" [S9]. The Pink Shinobi vs RockCrock match at Genesis settled it. |
| Brinstar | Rising acid let Jigglypuff "maintain extremely powerful control over the platforms with little to no risk". A ceiling glitch gives acid KOs at very low percents. The ledges favour floaty characters over wall-dependent recoveries [S10]. |
| Rainbow Cruise | Ungrabbable ledges, disappearing platforms and "winding, non-linear passages". It favours good air mobility, "overcentralizes keeping up with the stage instead of actually fighting", and its many paths promote stalling [S19]. |
| Corneria | Arwing lasers and the Great Fox's cannon are "extremely powerful stage hazard[s]". The fin works as a wall for Fox's shine infinites. It encourages camping [S14]. |
| Mute City | Few grabbable ledges, and a main platform that "can just barely provide sufficient space for two combatants". It lags in doubles. "Peach had a notorious advantage against Fox and Falco" [S15]. |
| Poké Floats | Stage spikes and collision problems from the moving floats. Fox timeouts with small vertical blast zones [S20]. |
| Green Greens | Camping, Bomb Blocks, side blast lines "abnormally close to the platforms", and wall infinites on the block tower [S21]. |
| Yoshi's Island | Close blast zones and a walk-off blast line. Often no bottom blast line, which "marginalizes edgeguarding". The rotating blocks work as a cave of life. Its ramps give Fox an unavoidable shine KO [S22]. |
| Jungle Japes | Favours jumpers and projectiles, with gimps on both sides. The Klaptrap deals 30% and can one-hit KO. An "incredibly high ceiling" [S23]. |
| Princess Peach's Castle | Permanent walls give shine infinites and "reduce how many angles players can attack each other". Banzai Bill disrupts [S24]. |

**The general rule** (SmashWiki's stage-legality criteria [S1]). Stages are banned for:
- extreme matchup imbalance ("Fox in Temple" camping);
- walk-off blast lines, which give "unreasonably powerful camping positions";
- caves of life;
- hazards that give easy KOs;
- glitches that can be exploited.

## 5. What this means for a new starter (carried into DESIGN.md §1)

The legal list sorts along one axis: **how much the stage, rather than the players, decides the game.**
- **Banned** stages decide games outright: hazards that kill, walls that enable infinites, walk-offs, stalling.
- **The counterpick** decides matchups: Stadium's size and low ceiling hand Fox and Falco a camping game, and its
  transformations interrupt play.
- **Starters** still lean. Dream Land favours floaty characters, Yoshi's Story fast fallers, Final Destination grapplers
  and projectiles. But each lean is **moderate, and another starter mirrors it**. Striking works because each player can
  remove their worst stage and still reach a fair one.

A sixth starter has to lean no further than the five do. It earns its place by offering a *different* lean, a new playmap
inside the same envelope, and never by adding an outlier.
